About this project

Political podcasts reach millions of people every week, and the hosts rarely talk about Democrats, Israel or immigration the same way. I’m Boaz Alkalay, a graduate student in Data Analytics and Computational Social Science at UMass Amherst, and I built this to measure those differences.

I transcribed about 88,000 episodes from more than 80 shows, from 2021 to now, and compared the words each show uses around a chosen term. Two shows can land close together on the map and still disagree completely. What they share is how they talk about the word, not what they think of it.

The data files (word lists, positions and short passages, no full transcripts) are published as a Hugging Face dataset. How to cite the project is at the bottom of every page.

How it works

Switch "Explanations" at the top between Plain and Technical for the level of detail.

What it measures

Pick a term, and the site looks at the words political podcasts use around it. It then measures how much those words differ from show to show and from one stretch of time to the next. This is about how shows talk about a word, which is a different thing from what they think of it. Two shows can use the same vocabulary around “Israel” and still take opposite sides.For a target term, the distribution of its local contexts per show and period, summarised as a mean à la carte (ALC) embedding (Rodriguez, Spirling and Stewart 2023), and noise-corrected distances between those means. Vocabulary and topic, not stance.

From episodes to a dot

Episodes are transcribed automatically from each show’s public feed. Every time the term comes up, we take the nouns, names and adjectives around it and turn them into numbers that stand for their meaning. An episode has to mention the term at least 3 times to count, and its mentions are averaged together. A show’s dot for a period is the average of its episodes, so one episode that says the word fifty times doesn’t drown out the rest. A show needs 8 of these episodes in a period to get a regular dot, and shows with 3 to 7 get a faded, rough one.Whisper medium (int8) transcripts; spaCy lemmatisation (NOUN, PROPN, ADJ); word2vec 100-d (window 5); ALC transform A learned on words with ≥ 10 uses (hold-out median cosine 0.875, round-2 model). Mention embedding = A × mean of ±5-token context vectors; episode = mean of its mentions (≥ 3); cell μ = mean over episodes, v = tr(cov)/n. Placed if n ≥ 8; 3-7 "thin".

The map

Each show’s point really sits in 100 dimensions, and the map squeezes that down to two, so some detail gets lost. A flat map of the globe has the same problem. It’s useful, but the distances on it aren’t exact. The layout is fitted once across all periods, which is why you can compare where a show sits from one period to the next. The numbers in the other sections use the full data, so trust them over distances you eyeball on the map.Classical MDS of all placed cells on noise-corrected squared distances (one joint frame); centred variant subtracts each period's equal-weight mean. Share of variation kept is shown under the map.

Measuring differences, and uncertainty

Even two random handfuls of episodes from the same show will look a little different, and the fewer episodes there are, the bigger that gap gets. We work out how big a gap chance alone would produce and take it out of every difference, so zero means no difference the data can pick up. Every number comes with a 90% range. If the range reaches zero, the difference can’t be told apart from chance. Shows with few episodes are marked rough, and their range comes from a formula instead of resampling. Each pair is checked on its own, so with hundreds of pairs, about 1 in 20 will look clearly different by chance alone. A single clear result deserves a closer look before you trust it.d² = |μ_a − μ_b|² − v_a − v_b, clipped at 0; distance from the group is leave-one-out against the equal-weight mean of the others. 90% percentile bootstrap over episodes with the noise term subtracted twice per draw (conservative near zero). Thin cells and the include toggle use an analytic score-type interval from v, d_eff and r1 (simulated coverage ≈ 90%). Intervals are per pair, not adjusted for multiple comparisons (no FDR/familywise correction).

The word chart: what the words mean

Each show’s way of talking about a term in an interval gets boiled down to one point in a space of meanings. Every mention of the term is turned into numbers from the words around it, and those numbers are averaged. The distance between two shows’ points is how different they are, given as one number with a range (“How different”). Nobody can read a point in that space directly, so the chart lists dictionary words that sit near each end. In the scatterplot, each dot is a word. Across is how close it is to the first show’s point, and up is how close it is to the second’s. Words on the diagonal are about as close to both. The further a word sits from the diagonal, the more it marks one show, and its colour tells you which. The words tell you what kind of difference it is, and the “How different” number tells you how big. They aren’t a word count either, and a show may never have said some of them. Read them together as landmarks, and treat the passages as the evidence. “Ranked bars” shows the same thing as a list of the strongest words on each side.Candidate words are the union of each side's 150 nearest vocabulary words (cosine to the side's mean ALC vector; ≥ 50 corpus uses; the term itself excluded, so related forms such as "democratic" can appear). Each is ranked by the ratio cos(w, μ_B) / cos(w, μ_A); words with a non-positive cosine on either side are dropped. Scatterplot: x = cos(w, μ_A), y = cos(w, μ_B) on log scales, so lines of constant ratio (1.5×, 2×) run parallel to the diagonal; the 30 words with the largest |log2 ratio| per side plus 15 near-equal words (|log2| ≤ 0.1, ranked by √(cos_A·cos_B)); colour = log2 ratio; labels placed by |log2 ratio| without overlaps. The ranking describes the direction separating μ_A and μ_B; its size is the noise-corrected distance with its bootstrap interval ("How different"). Words are selected for contrast, so they separate the sides by construction: use them to direct reading, not as evidence. Show vs the rest: the same, against the leave-one-out equal-weight mean of the other placed shows.

Passages and AI labels

The passages are real stretches of transcript around actual mentions of the term. They’re picked by ranking, so you see the clearest cases, and they aren’t a random sample. The short labels at the edges of the map were written by an AI model (Claude) from the list of words at each edge. They describe a direction on the map, and they aren’t a description of any one podcast.Passages: mentions ranked by distance to μ or by projection on the contrast or drift direction, one per episode. AI direction interpretations: Claude (claude-sonnet-5-5), prompt stored with the output, cached; only the word lists are sent.

How the terms were chosen

I picked the terms by hand, as examples. They are not a sample of political vocabulary, so the results describe these words only, not how the shows talk about politics in general. I chose them to cover different kinds of topics: foreign affairs (iran, israel, ukraine, gaza), immigration and the economy (immigration, tariff), political labels (trump, democrat, socialist, maga), scandal (epstein), religion (muslim) and social issues (abortion). Each term also had to come up often enough to map: enough shows with 8+ episodes that use it 3+ times in a period. The last seven were written down before I looked at any of their results. Only single words work, so phrases such as "the left" can't be terms.Purposive, researcher-chosen terms (not sampled): spread across topic types, then screened for coverage (periods with 5+ placed shows at 91 days). The second batch (immigration, tariff, gaza, epstein, maga, muslim, abortion) was fixed before any results were seen; islam was replaced by muslim on coverage alone (median 0 placed shows), before mapping. Matching is on single lemmatized tokens (democrat ≠ democratic; multi-word phrases unsupported). Findings do not generalise beyond the chosen terms.

How groups were chosen

The groups are my judgment, not a fact about the shows. I wrote a one-line rule for each group first, then placed each show by things outside this data (its network, how its hosts describe themselves, its format), never by where it sits on the map. A show can be in more than one group. The rules:Researcher-assigned groups (reviewed 2026-11-12). Rule written per group before placement; placement from external criteria (network, self-description, format), not from the embedding. Groups may overlap; two groups compared with each other share no shows. A group needs 2+ placed shows in a period to get a group point. Rules:

  • Nationalist / Populist Right: Hosts who put national identity or anti-establishment populism at the centre, outside the established conservative networks.
  • Conservative Media: Large-audience conservative commentary shows, broadly supportive of Trump, inside established conservative media.
  • Daily Wire: Shows produced by The Daily Wire, during their Daily Wire years.
  • Anti-Trump Right: Right-leaning or formerly right-leaning hosts whose shows are mainly critical of Trump.
  • Conservatives Trump criticised (Apr 9, 2026): Conservative hosts whom Trump named in his post of April 9, 2026.
  • Centrist / Heterodox: Hosts who do not consistently take one party's side, or whose format is explicitly cross-partisan.
  • Liberal: Center-left commentary, broadly aligned with the Democratic Party.
  • Progressive / Leftist: Commentary from the left, more adversarial than mainstream liberal shows.
  • Christian: Shows whose main lens is Christian faith, whatever their politics.
  • Men's Self-Improvement: Shows mainly about men's self-improvement.
  • Mainstream News: News programs made by news organizations, in a reporting format rather than one host's commentary.
Limits

The transcripts are automatic, so they have mistakes, especially with names (Mamdani sometimes comes out as “mandamis”). Shows joined the data at different times. 39 of the 83 have episodes from 2021 and 60 by 2025, so the earlier periods compare fewer shows. The newest period isn’t finished yet. With this many possible comparisons, some differences will look interesting by chance alone, so treat them as places to start reading.Composition changes over time (39 of 83 shows present by mid-2021, 47 by 2022-07, 60 by 2025-01); partial last period; multiple comparisons across terms × pairs; AI labels unvalidated; map share of variation median 56% (27-100%) across the 2026-11-12 bundles.
